Two conventions, so a reader can use any line without opening this file's closer first. The prose
carries the date of the thing measured; the citation carries the class of the source and the year
that source was published, which are often different years and are meant to be. And a figure a
company reports about itself is labelled as such, because the subject of a claim is not a neutral
witness to it.

## Hypotheses

Strategic readings, not facts. Each one is a conclusion somebody could argue with, restated here in
this pack's own words so that a run can test it rather than inherit it.

- [HYPOTHESIS] The gap between a revenue line that roughly doubled and a mark that barely moved is the whole question about this company, and public data cannot resolve it: either nobody has repriced the business since 2021, or the usage picture behind the headline would not hold a higher price.
- [HYPOTHESIS] A flat mark set only by a secondary sale reads as a weak indicator, not as a verdict from the market, and the category as a whole repriced below its 2021 peaks, so some of the flatness belongs to the category and not to the company.
- [HYPOTHESIS] The single most decisive number about this company is one it has never published, namely how many people work inside the product in a given day or month and how many customers pay for it.
- [HYPOTHESIS] A company that publishes revenue, plan mix and adoption rates while withholding active usage is more likely withholding the less flattering number than the less interesting one.
- [HYPOTHESIS] A generous free tier makes a registered-user headline a weak proxy for dependence: registrations count people who arrived, not people whose work would break if the product disappeared.
- [HYPOTHESIS] Attributing about half of recurring revenue to plans that bundle AI reports plan mix rather than willingness to pay for AI, and the difference between those two readings is the difference between a pricing story and a product story.
- [HYPOTHESIS] The bet behind the developer platform is that a workspace becomes the system agents read from and write to; the direction is defensible and the economics are still unproven, because third-party workers do not begin billing until late 2026 and no paid marketplace exists yet.
- [HYPOTHESIS] The published customer stories are the strongest evidence available for that bet and are also the weakest kind of evidence: hand-picked accounts, self-reported numbers, no cohort behind any of them, and no comparable set that chose a different tool.
- [HYPOTHESIS] The pattern worth watching across those stories is who builds the agents, namely staff with no engineering background, which is the same move this product made once before when it handed non-engineers a database.
- [HYPOTHESIS] Whether that move generalises past design-literate startups is the open question, and it is precisely the thing no published number covers.
- [HYPOTHESIS] The company's own survey, reporting that most organizations use AI as an assistant and almost none at the deepest level, is evidence against the depth its positioning implies, and it is more credible for coming from the vendor.
- [HYPOTHESIS] A read of the public surface ranks build quality and adaptability highest, with shared-team working close behind, and puts predictability of cost, clarity of pricing and clarity of scope at the bottom - an ordering that is an editorial judgement rather than a measurement, which is why this pack carries no scored instrument of its own: a scale invented here would read as one.
- [HYPOTHESIS] Metered AI credits sit underneath the weakest end of that ordering: a plan price that does not predict a monthly bill is a pricing problem before it is a product one.
- [HYPOTHESIS] What would settle the open question is narrow and checkable: one published active-usage or retention figure that is strong, and evidence that third-party agents produce real revenue once billing starts.

## Evidence bar

This pack is public information about a named company, assembled wholly from the public record with
no access inside the company. That sets a ceiling
on what any line here can do, and the ceiling is worth stating plainly rather than discovering later.

- FOUND here means published somewhere public and read as published. It does not mean audited, and
  for a private company most of it never will be. Every FOUND line names what kind of figure it is
  in the same bracket as the tag, because a company-stated attach rate and a third-party estimate of
  revenue are not the same species of number.
- Source classes, never source names. Figures carry a class and a year rather than an outlet,
  which is this library's standing convention for anonymized descriptors. The class is the honest
  unit: it tells a reader how much authority the figure carries without implying that one named
  outlet verified it.
- Self-reported is labelled. Every customer figure in this pack comes from a page the vendor
  published about a customer who agreed to appear on it. Those are a floor on what is possible and
  say nothing about the median account.
- CALCULATED lines carry their arithmetic inline, and every ratio inside them draws its inputs from
  one source class on one basis. Where a line reports two ratios, each is worked separately and
  neither is divided into the other, because their classes differ. Dividing a third-party revenue
  estimate by a company-stated user count would cross two sources and two definitions, so this pack
  does not do it, and neither should a run reading it.
- HYPOTHESIS lines are readings, not findings. They are here to be tested against the operator's
  own evidence, and none of them may travel into a delivered document as a fact.

This pack is trusted only when all of the following hold.

1. No figure here is ever cited as a fact about the operator's own company. It is a band, and a band
   is a comparison rather than a target.
2. Estimates are never restated as disclosures. One revenue figure in this pack is company-confirmed
   and the rest of the revenue line is somebody's model of a private company.
3. Population before precision. The attach rates are denominated on paying customers, the reach
   figures on registered users, and the case-study numbers on one self-selected account each. Three
   different populations, and no two of them belong on one chart.
4. The plan-based revenue attribution is never restated as AI usage, in either direction.
5. The named absences stay named. Active usage, paid-customer count, retention and the AI-driven
   revenue split are the four numbers this benchmark cannot supply, and they are exactly the four a
   reader most wants. Each is recorded above against the operator's own data, and filling one with
   the nearest adjacent figure from another company would be the single fastest way to turn this
   pack into a source of confident errors.
